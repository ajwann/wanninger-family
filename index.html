The crossing · spring 1884

Antwerp to New York on the Westernland

A National Archives index and the original manifest place Fanny and five children on the Red Star Line steamer Westernland, which reached New York on 11 June 1884. A gardener listed with them is almost certainly John.

The ship

The Westernland was barely seven months into service. Laird Brothers of Birkenhead launched her on 4 August 1883, and she left Antwerp on her maiden voyage that November. She was the Red Star Line’s first steel-hulled ship and its first with two funnels, and she still carried four masts with sails as a backup to her single compound steam engine. At about 14.5 knots she was a modern, middle-sized emigrant liner, built for exactly the kind of family travelling in her lower decks.

The Red Star Line was a Belgian-American company that ran from Antwerp. For Bavarians, Antwerp was a real alternative to Bremen and Hamburg, because it could be reached by rail through the Rhineland.

Life in steerage Typical for the period

No account of this particular voyage survives in the family records, so what follows is a description of steerage travel in the early 1880s, not a report of what the Wanningers saw. Their manifest shows they travelled in steerage, the cheapest class.

Steerage meant open compartments below the main deck, lined with two-tier bunks. Single men were usually berthed apart from families and single women. Passengers often had to bring their own mattress, blanket, plate, cup and spoon, and many bought them at the port. Air came through hatches and ventilators, and in rough weather the hatches were closed. Washing was mostly in cold salt water.

The line supplied the food, served at long tables or carried down in mess tins. A steerage passenger writing in July 1884 about another steamer listed coffee sweetened with molasses and bread or porridge in the morning, then soup, salt beef or salt pork and potatoes at midday, with a pudding on Sundays. That writer called the food the worst part of the trip. Seasickness flattened many people for the first days. In good weather, steerage passengers crowded onto their section of open deck, where there was singing, cards and talk in a dozen languages.

At the Westernland’s speed the crossing would have taken roughly ten to twelve days. For Fanny, 51, keeping five children aged 9 to 16 fed and together in a crowded compartment was likely the hardest part of the voyage.

Arrival

In 1884 New York had no Ellis Island. Immigrants landed at Castle Garden, the state’s station at the Battery on the tip of Manhattan, where names were taken, money could be changed and railroad tickets bought. From there, a family bound for Cincinnati would normally go by train, a trip of a day or more.

Bavaria · 1640 to 1884

Eight generations near Cham, and why the family left

The parish books place the family in one small corner of the Upper Palatinate, in eastern Bavaria close to the Bohemian border, from the 1640s to John’s generation.

The line in the parish books Documented

The registers of Schorndorf parish, which begin in 1639, trace the line through eight generations, each linked to the next by name in a record. In two and a half centuries the family moved only a few kilometres: from Unteraigen to Penting in 1689, and from Penting to Untertraubenbach about 1774. All nineteen entries are in Documents.

  1. 1640–1643 · UnteraigenGeorg Wäning and Magdalena have Anna and Johann baptized, in the last decade of the Thirty Years’ War.
  2. 1665–1695 · UnteraigenJohann marries Elisabeth Stelzl of Thierling and farms at Unteraigen. Their son Thomas is baptized in 1669.
  3. 1689–1713 · PentingThomas marries Anna Veldtbauer, a widow, and farms at Penting. After her death he marries Maria Knod. He has a son Georg in 1696 and a Johann Georg in 1701.
  4. 1722–1747 · PentingOne of those two Georgs, and it is not settled which, marries Dorothea, a widow, and farms at Penting. Their son Benedikt is baptized in 1724.
  5. 1747–1774 · PentingBenedikt marries Barbara Häbl, a shoemaker’s daughter from the village. By 1774 they are half-farmers.
  6. 1774–1801 · UntertraubenbachTheir son Thomas marries Susanna Greßlinger of Untertraubenbach, and the family settles in her village.
  7. 1801–1830 · UntertraubenbachMichael Wanninger and Margaretha Winkel marry as cottagers. Their son Leonhard is born in 1807.

Penting is a small church village now in the municipality of Schorndorf, about 3.5 km from Untertraubenbach; these books are those of the Untertraubenbach branch of Schorndorf parish, which covered both villages.

Where John probably came from Likely

The last step rests on a family tree rather than a record. Johann Baptist Wanninger was baptized on 2 February 1836 at Untertraubenbach, house no. 15. His father Leonhard was a Söldner, the holder of a Sölde: a cottage with a small piece of land, usually too little to feed a family without extra work. His mother was Theres Reil, and an older brother, Michael, was born in 1831 and married at home in 1866. A family tree made by others links this Johann to the Cincinnati gardener of the same age, and the dates fit, but no record yet ties the two together.

The family’s children were not found in the baptism books of a dozen parishes around Cham or in Regensburg, so they were probably born somewhere else in Bavaria. George’s 1930 death certificate is the only family record that names Bavaria, and none names a town.

Two and a half centuries on the land Interpretation

The registers’ own words for the family show their land shrinking. Johann, Thomas and Georg are each called a farmer, agricola, between 1689 and 1747. Benedikt and Barbara are half-farmers in 1774, and from Michael’s wedding in 1801 the family are cottagers. Bavarian farms usually passed whole to one child, so the others had to marry into a holding, as Thomas and Georg both seem to have done by marrying widows, or make do with less. By the time John was born, his father held a cottage and a small piece of land.

The earlier generations also lived through harder times. Georg and Magdalena raised their children in the Thirty Years’ War. The country around Cham had passed to Bavaria in 1628, and Swedish armies, plague and soldiers quartered on farms emptied holdings across the region before peace came in 1648. Thomas farmed at Penting when Austria occupied Bavaria from 1704 and crushed a peasants’ rising in 1705. Leonhard was born in 1807, a year after Bavaria became a kingdom and a year before it abolished serfdom.

Daily life Typical for the region

Untertraubenbach sits in the Regen valley among small farming villages. In the decades John grew up, life followed the parish church and the farm year: rye, oats, potatoes and flax on small fields, a cow or a few goats, winter work spinning, weaving or cutting wood. Sons of cottagers like Leonhard often became farmhands, day labourers or craftsmen, because the family plot could not be split again. A trained gardener, as John was in Cincinnati, could find work on estates, in towns, or in market gardens supplying them.

The region changed around him. In 1848 Bavaria ended the old feudal dues peasants owed their lords. In 1861 a railway reached Cham, which made travel cheaper and opened the district to outside goods. In 1866 Bavaria fought on Austria’s side against Prussia and lost, and in January 1871 it joined the new German Empire. William, born on 18 January 1871, arrived the same day the empire was proclaimed at Versailles.

Why they left Interpretation

No letter or record gives the family’s own reason. The pattern of what is known, set against what drove the wider emigration of the 1880s, points to several likely causes.

  • A relative already in CincinnatiFanny’s brother August Mayer, a marble cutter, lived in Cincinnati and left her everything in his 1892 will. Most emigrants followed someone they knew, and this is the most likely reason they chose that city.
  • No land to inheritThe registers show the family’s holding shrinking, from farmers in the 1600s to cottagers by 1801. A younger son of a cottager had little prospect of owning a farm, and contemporary reports put land hunger and low rural wages at the top of the reasons Germans left.
  • Falling farm pricesFrom the 1870s, cheap grain from America and Russia pushed prices down across Europe, squeezing small holders and the people who worked for them.
  • Sons near military ageGeorge was 16 and William 13. Leaving before the boys were called up for three years’ service was a common motive in the 1880s. This is a general pattern, not something recorded for this family.
  • Part of the largest waveGerman emigration surged after 1880 to its highest level ever. Almost three million Germans left between 1862 and 1890, most of them for the United States.
  • A mother willing to start over at 51Fanny was past fifty when she left. Moving with five children that late in life suggests the family saw little future at home for the young ones.

    Glossary

    Words from the records

    The old registers are in Latin and German, and genealogy has shorthand of its own. These are the terms used on this page.

    agricola, colonus, rusticus
    Latin for a farmer. In these books it means a man with a full farm.
    ½ colonus, half-farmer
    A farmer with about half a full holding, as Benedikt was at Penting in 1774.
    Söldner, domuncularius
    A cottager: the holder of a Sölde, a small house with little or no land, who usually also worked for others or at a trade.
    juvenis
    A young, unmarried man.
    vidua, viduus
    A widow; a widower.
    patrinus, patrina
    Godfather; godmother.
    uxor, coniuges
    Wife; a married couple.
    filius legitimus, filia legitima
    Legitimate son; legitimate daughter: born to a married couple.
    ledig Standts
    German, “of single status”: unmarried. From Johann’s 1665 wedding.
    8bris
    October, written as the eighth month of the old Roman year.
    fl.
    Floruit, “flourished”: the years someone appears in records, when birth and death are unknown.
    b., m., c.
    Born; married; circa, about.
    (?)
    A reading of the handwriting that is uncertain.
    3× great-grandfather
    The number of “greats”: Lawrence’s 3× great-grandfather is his great-great-great-grandfather.
    Manifest
    A ship’s passenger list, made at the port of departure and handed in on arrival.
    Naturalization
    Becoming a citizen. Children who arrived under eighteen could be admitted on simpler terms.
    Upper Palatinate, Oberpfalz
    The region of eastern Bavaria around Cham and Regensburg.
    Parish register, Matrikel
    The church’s books of baptisms, marriages and burials, the only records of ordinary villagers before the 1800s.
